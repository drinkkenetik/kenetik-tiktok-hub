The full script, start to finish

Meet Jen. She’s forty-nine, and she runs the meeting, the family calendar and half the group chats. But lately, she hasn’t felt like herself, and she can’t figure out why. Then, in the middle of a sentence, a word she’s used a thousand times just disappears. Everyone’s waiting.

It happens again on Thursday, and she starts to wonder if this is just how it is now. It’s perimenopause. Everyone talks about hot flashes. Nobody mentioned this part.

So she looks it up, and finds a thousand menopause products. Gummies, powders, teas, every one promising to fix it. Mostly noise. So she pours another coffee. Now her heart’s racing, she’s up at 2am, and the word still isn’t there.

Here’s what nobody explains. Your brain is small, but it uses about 20% of your body’s energy, and it runs mostly on sugar. In 2025, scientists studied brain scans from over 19,000 people to find out when the brain starts to age. The answer was midlife, starting in our forties. And it pointed to fuel: brain cells get worse at using sugar.

So Jen isn’t losing it. Her brain is just short on fuel it can use. The good news is, the brain has a second fuel: ketones. Brain cells can use them without insulin, and the more ketones in your blood, the more your brain uses. When the same scientists gave people ketones, their brain networks steadied, especially in midlife.

The catch? Your body only makes a lot of ketones if you fast for days, and Jen has a job. So she drinks Kenetik. Ketones on demand, with no fasting and no diet change. Twelve grams in every can, and no caffeine, so nothing’s keeping her up at 2am.

Clear head. Jen finishes her sentence and carries on. No promises, it’s not a cure. Nothing was wrong with Jen. She was running on empty.

The look

Premium, warm and calm. Think a beautifully designed wellness studio, not a hospital and not an energy drink ad. Golden, directional light, warm wood and stone, room to breathe, and one focal point per frame.

Photoreal objects, no people. Our 3D video never shows people, faces or hands, because made-up people are an obvious tell. Tell Jen’s story through her things: the laptop, the phone, the calendar, the coffee, the can.

One object for the science. Jen’s brain as a small engine with two fuel lines, clean and simple. No anatomy, no cartoon hormones, nothing that looks like a pamphlet or a clinic.

Brand colours, used lightly. Mostly warm neutrals. Blueberry blue (#269dd2) is the one accent, for the ketone line. The plum-to-sky-blue gradient (#7B2A86 to #2A9AD3) stays on the end card.

Slow, steady motion. Smooth camera moves, soft light changes, and the diagonal of the can’s chevron as the transition. No fast cuts, bouncing props, spinning clocks, confetti, glow or neon.

What our community responds to: our best-received explainers are simple, calm and a little dry, like your Golden Pear video. One idea per scene, said plainly, beats spectacle.

Every version

Do

  • Tell Jen’s story through her world: the meeting, the phone, the calendar, the coffee, the can.
  • Use a female narrator: warm, dry and unhurried, like a friend telling you about Jen, never an announcer.
  • Make the lost word visible: a cursor that stops mid-word and blinks, then finishes it in the payoff.
  • Clear the haze on the sip, and make the can’s crack the loudest sound in the video.
  • Burn the on-screen text in, big and readable. Most people watch with the sound off.
  • Deliver 9:16 first, with a 60-second cutdown.

Don't

  • Call it an energy drink or a supplement. It’s brain fuel.
  • Put a hormone patch, a prescription or a doctor in the noise scene. The noise is products, never medical care.
  • Show people, faces or hands, or turn Jen into a mascot. She lives in the narration and her things.
  • Open in a kitchen. The first frame is Jen’s world mid-task: the meeting, the list, the phone.
  • Add a before-and-after or a transformation montage. The sentence finishes and the meeting moves on.
  • Say “your menopause,” or guess the viewer’s age. Jen’s age is Jen’s.
