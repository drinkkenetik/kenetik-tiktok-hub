Matt Hughes hooks — Simple hooks for Matt — the basics

8 simple hooks  ·  GENERAL
The north star

Lead with the problem or the good feeling, not caffeine or ketones. Kenetik is brain fuel. These lines are written short and plain so Matt can say them easily and naturally. Pick one, say it like you talk, and film it in the first two seconds.

The hooks
01
“Your brain runs on fuel. When it runs low, you feel foggy. This keeps me sharp.”
Problem / brain fuel · Direct to camera · Broad
Fog is something the viewer already noticed about their own afternoon, so naming it first makes the rest sound like an explanation rather than a pitch. Coming from you it lands harder because nobody expects a sales voice from you, and you should not use one. Say it flat and slow.
02
“Coffee gives you a spike, then a crash. Kenetik keeps me steady. No caffeine.”
Contrast / stimulant switch · Direct to camera · Coffee drinkers
You are not asking anyone to give up coffee, you are describing a swap for one part of the day, which is a far smaller ask than it sounds. Caffeine-swap content converts around 12 percent for us. Keep the tone even, with no dig at coffee.
03
“I drink this when I need to focus. It keeps my head clear.”
Simple testimonial · POV · Broad
There is nothing here for a viewer to argue with, because you are reporting what you do rather than claiming what it does. That gap is exactly why understatement works for you: your record already did the persuading, so the line only has to be true. One take.
04
“This is brain fuel. It helps me think clearly. And it has no caffeine.”
Category stake · Direct to camera · Broad
People file a can into a category within a second of seeing it, so telling them what it is beats letting them guess wrong. Brain fuel and caffeine-free are both approved facts and both do work in one short sentence. Hold the can in frame while you say it.
05
“Foggy in the afternoon? That is your brain low on fuel. Try this.”
Callout / problem · Direct to camera · Broad
A question with a time of day attached gets answered in the viewer's head before they decide whether to keep watching, and once they have answered yes they are already in the video. The afternoon detail is what makes it land. Vague callouts get scrolled past.
06
“One drink and my focus comes back. I take one when I start to fade.”
Simple benefit · POV · Broad
Focus is our second-best converting theme at about 13 percent, and this is the plainest way to say it. Notice you are describing what happens for you, not promising what will happen for them. Keep it in first person, which is both truer and stronger from you.
07
“Big names drink Kenetik. There is a reason. It works.”
Social proof · Direct to camera · Broad
Proof works better when it is specific, so name someone you can actually name instead of leaving it at big names. The line also carries an implied filter: people assume you would not put your name beside something ordinary. Say who, then stop talking.
08
“I keep one in the fridge every day. It is part of my routine now.”
Ritual · POV · Loyal fans
A shelf in your fridge says more than a recommendation does. Sponsored posts describe a product, habits describe a life, and viewers can tell the difference in about a second. Film it where the can actually lives rather than staged on a clean counter.
Do's and don'ts

Do

  • Say "brain fuel" once.
  • Open with the problem or feeling in the first few seconds.
  • Keep it real and simple, like you talk.
  • Show yourself drinking it.
  • Stick to how it feels.

Don't

  • Call it an energy drink.
  • Make any health or medical claim.
  • Open with caffeine or ketones.
  • Over-produce it.
  • Cram everything into the opener.

The hooks
01
“New Kenetik flavor is here. It will sell out fast. I am grabbing mine now.”
Drop / scarcity · Unboxing or first-sip · Broad
You are standing in line rather than behind the counter, and nobody feels sold to by another buyer. That flip is the whole mechanism here. Scarcity only holds if it is true, so film it the day the flavor lands, not a week after the noise has passed.
02
“I love the taste of this new flavor. And it keeps my head clear.”
Taste + brain fuel · First-sip · Broad
Taste is our best-converting theme at about 16 percent and earns roughly three times what our weakest angle does. Leading with flavor and letting the clear-head line ride second gives the viewer a reason to want it and a reason to justify it. Keep that order.
03
“New flavor, same reason I drink it. Clean focus, no caffeine.”
Problem-first · Direct to camera · Broad
Same reason I drink it quietly establishes that you were drinking this before there was anything new to promote. That continuity is worth more than any adjective about the flavor. Do not oversell the newness — the calm version reads truer from you than excitement would.
04
“Kenetik flavors sell out fast. Get this one before it is gone.”
Scarcity · Direct to camera · FOMO
Missing out costs a viewer more, psychologically, than gaining something gains them, which is why sell-out framing moves faster than a feature line. It only stays credible because it has actually happened. Say it the way you would tell a friend, not the way an ad would.
05
“First taste of the new flavor. Here is what I think.”
First taste · First-sip reaction · Broad
You are promising a verdict rather than a product, so the viewer stays to find out which way you land. That works only if there is genuine suspense on your face. If you already know you love it, pick a different line, and do not trim the first second of the reaction.
06
“This new flavor is really good. It is brain fuel that tastes great.”
Taste + brain fuel · First-sip · Broad
Most people assume anything sold as functional tastes like medicine, so calling it good is answering an objection they never voiced. Put the sip before the sentence and let your face confirm it before your words do. Do not narrate over the drink.
07
“Want the new flavor? Get it now. It will not last long.”
Urgency · Direct to camera · Broad
A question and an instruction in one breath leaves the viewer nothing to do but decide, which makes this a closer more than an opener. If you are going to point at anything, say tap the orange cart out loud: the 37 videos that ever have convert at 43 percent against a 10 percent baseline.
08
“New flavor just dropped. I am trying it for the first time. Watch.”
Unboxing / POV · POV · Broad
Firsts cannot be repeated and viewers know it, so the promise of a real first reaction is enough to hold them through the sip. Raw one-take footage beat polished ad-style content in every brand we audited. Shoot this on your phone in whatever light is in the room.

First taste / reaction · First-sip · Broad
Trying it first gives the viewer something they cannot get anywhere else yet, and a reaction is the one payoff nobody can spoil in the comments. Point the camera at your face rather than the can for the opening beat. Keep whatever you actually said.

Taste · First-sip · Broad

Taste + brain fuel · First-sip · Broad

“Raspberry Lychee is here. It will sell out fast. Get it now.”
Scarcity · Direct to camera · FOMO
Scarcity is more persuasive on a flavor than on a product, because flavors genuinely do disappear and people have been burned before. Say it plainly and move on. The moment you add pressure to the delivery it stops sounding like you, and the credibility is the asset.
05
“First sip of Raspberry Lychee. Watch my face.”
First-sip reaction · POV · Broad
Watch my face promises evidence rather than opinion, and evidence is harder to scroll past. It also removes any need for you to describe the flavor, which suits you, since describing is not your register. Do not perform it — a flat reaction is still information.
06
“I did not expect to love this. Raspberry Lychee is really good.”
Honest reaction · First-sip · Broad
Leading with your own doubt disarms theirs. Bloom built its best videos on taste disbelief, and it works because viewers assume a sponsored opinion was decided in advance. Admitting you expected less is the fastest way out of that assumption. Only use it if it is true.
07
“Brain fuel that tastes like Raspberry Lychee? Yes, please.”
Taste + brain fuel · Direct to camera · Broad
This is lighter than your usual register, which is exactly why it can work. A plain-spoken man sounding quietly pleased is more convincing than enthusiasm from someone who always sounds enthusiastic. Do not push it. Say it dry and let the line carry the fun.
08
“Raspberry Lychee drops Friday. Get it before it is gone.”
Drop / urgency · Direct to camera · Broad
A date gives the viewer something to do besides decide right now, and it makes the sell-out line concrete instead of atmospheric. Post it the day before as well as the day of. The same sentence does different work on each side of a drop.

Matt Hughes hooks — Simple value hooks (never discount)

8 simple hooks  ·  GETTING PAST PRICE
The north star

Some people see the price as a hump before they feel the value. Never discount. Make the value obvious. These are short, plain value reframes Matt can deliver in one breath.

The hooks
01
“It costs a few dollars a day. For a clear head, that is worth it.”
Cost-per-day reframe · Direct to camera · Broad
Dividing the price into a day makes it comparable to something the viewer already buys without thinking, which is the whole job. You are not defending the number, you are changing the unit it is measured in. Never answer price with a discount here. Say the figure and let it sit.
02
“It replaced my afternoon coffee. And I focus better. Easy choice.”
Replaces coffee · Direct to camera · Coffee drinkers
Replacement beats addition. Someone doing the math on a new expense says no, but someone swapping one thing for another has already budgeted for it. Caffeine-swap content converts at about 12 percent for us, and this framing is the reason it does.
03
“This does the job of coffee, energy drinks, and pre-workout. For less.”
Replaces the stack · Direct to camera · Broad
Stacking what it replaced on your own shelf makes the price read as consolidation rather than an addition. Watch the wording: you are describing what you personally stopped buying, not placing Kenetik in the energy drink category. Keep the whole line in the past tense and first person.
04
“I do not cheap out on what I use every day. My brain earns it.”
Worth / identity · Direct to camera · Value-minded
This is an identity line rather than a value line. It invites the viewer to decide what kind of buyer they are instead of arguing about a number, and from a farmer it reads as practical rather than indulgent. That is the version that converts. Do not smile through it.
05
“One good, focused afternoon is worth more than this costs.”
ROI of focus · Direct to camera · Broad
You are moving the comparison off the shelf and onto the afternoon, where the price obviously loses. Nothing about the product has to be argued for that to work. Keep the sentence exactly this short — every extra word turns it into a justification.
06
“You are paying for real brain fuel. You feel where the money goes.”
Quality · Direct to camera · Considered
Premium framing only works coming from someone with no reason to flatter the product, which is the position you are in. Stay on your own experience: you feel it, not they will feel it. Hold the can up so the viewer has something to attach the price to.
07
“I tried the cheaper drinks. This is the one I actually feel.”
Premium-because-it-works · POV · Broad
Admitting you shopped around is what makes the conclusion credible. Viewers assume anyone endorsing something was simply handed it, and this line closes that door before they open it. Name the cheaper ones only if you are comfortable doing so on camera.
08
“This is not a splurge. It is my daily brain fuel.”
Ritual close · POV · Broad
Naming the objection and dismissing it in the same breath is faster than arguing against it. Splurge is the exact word a hesitant viewer is using in their head, so hearing it from you ends the debate early. Strong closer, weak opener — put it last.

Matt Hughes hooks — Simple hooks for when Matt gives a big name a Kenetik

8 simple hooks  ·  PITCHING TO A CELEBRITY
The north star

Matt has access to big names. When he hands a celebrity a Kenetik and explains it in one short line, that is gold. Lead with the person and the moment, and film the reaction. Fill in the [name] where you see it.

The hooks
01
“I gave [name] a Kenetik today. Here is what they said.”
Celebrity pitch / reaction · Film the moment · Broad
Someone else's verdict is proof, your own is an opinion, and that difference is the entire reason this format beats anything you can say about the product yourself. Get the person's OK before you post their reaction. Film it in one take — a second attempt reads instantly.
02
“Watch me pitch brain fuel to [name] in ten seconds.”
Quick pitch · Film the moment · Broad
The bet the viewer makes is whether you can pull it off, not whether the drink is good, so they stay for the outcome and the product sells sideways while they watch. Set a real ten seconds and hold yourself to it. Clear the post with them first.
03
“I handed [name] the new flavor. They loved it.”
Reaction · Film the moment · Broad
A third party's taste reaction is the strongest proof we have. Taste converts at about 16 percent for us, and a stranger's face is far more persuasive than your description of a flavor. Show the sip. Never post a named reaction without asking that person first.
04
“I told [name] this is brain fuel with no caffeine. Here is their face.”
Pitch + reaction · Film the moment · Broad
The product fact is the surprise, and their expression makes the case instead of your voice. Two people in frame also makes the video feel overheard rather than performed, which lowers the viewer's guard. Ask permission before posting, and keep your pitch to the one sentence.
05
“Giving a famous friend their first Kenetik. Real reaction.”
First taste / celebrity · Film the moment · Broad
Leaving the name out lowers the permission bar and keeps the curiosity intact, since people will watch to work out who it is. A first reaction cannot be repeated, which is why it holds attention. If their face is in frame, you still need their OK.
06
“I pitch Kenetik to everyone I meet. Even the big names. Here is one.”
Persona / pitch · Direct to camera · Broad
This turns a one-off into a running bit, and a bit is something viewers come back for rather than stumble across. It also explains why you keep appearing with a can without ever sounding like an ad read. Only start it if you plan to make more than one.
07
“One sip and [name] got it. That is brain fuel.”
Reaction close · Film the moment · Broad
Got it is a small phrase doing heavy work — it implies the product explained itself and no pitch was needed, which is the most flattering thing you can say about anything you sell. Use it as the closing line over their reaction, and clear the post with them.
08
“I brought Kenetik to [name]. Watch what happened.”
Story / reaction · Film the moment · Broad
Watch what happened is an open loop with a person attached, and that is harder to scroll past than a loop about a drink. The payoff has to be real: a mild reaction is fine and still true, a manufactured one is obvious. Always get their approval before posting.

Focus wins · Direct to camera · Fighters

Edge / focus · Direct to camera · Fighters

Train + fuel · Direct to camera · Athletes

“Focus is everything in the cage. This keeps mine locked in.”
Focus · Direct to camera · Fighters
Everything in the cage is a phrase only someone who has been in one can use without sounding borrowed, and viewers hear that difference immediately. Keep the second half in first person. This is a focus line and nothing else — do not let it drift anywhere near health.

Category / fighters · First-sip · Fighters

Authority · Direct to camera · Fighters
